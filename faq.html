Frequently asked.

No. You do not have to start as a SOC analyst to build a cybersecurity career.

The two most common entry pathways we have seen are:

  • The technical or SOC pathway
  • The Governance, Risk, and Compliance pathway

The best path depends on your interests, strengths, experience, and career goals. Refer to our career roadmaps to determine which path may be the right fit for you.

Absolutely not.

If your goal is to work in Governance, Risk, and Compliance, the most efficient route is usually to pursue GRC directly. You do not need to develop all the technical investigation skills required of a SOC analyst before beginning GRC work.

Instead, focus on skills directly related to GRC, including:

  • Risk assessments
  • Security policies and controls
  • Regulatory and compliance frameworks
  • Auditing and evidence collection
  • Business communication
  • Report writing

Technical knowledge can strengthen your abilities as a GRC professional, but working in a SOC is not a prerequisite.

No. Help desk experience is not a universal requirement for breaking into cybersecurity.

Working at a help desk does not automatically lead to a cybersecurity position. We have seen people with 10 years of help desk or IT administration experience who still struggle to make the transition because they have not developed or demonstrated the security-specific skills employers want.

However, help desk experience can still be valuable. It can help you develop troubleshooting, networking, operating system, customer service, and documentation skills. It may also give you a competitive advantage if your local market has very few entry-level cybersecurity opportunities.

Whether taking a help desk position is a smart move depends on your circumstances. If you want help evaluating your situation, join the community and talk it through with us.

No. You do not need to collect a large number of certifications.

For someone pursuing a SOC analyst position, we generally recommend two certifications or equivalent forms of training:

  1. A foundational security certification. Security+ is often our preferred option because it is commonly recognized and frequently appears in cybersecurity job listings.
  2. A hands-on SOC certification or training program. Options include CyberDefenders, TryHackMe, Hack The Box Academy, and Blue Team Level 1.

These programs can all help you develop practical skills. The right one depends on your learning preferences, budget, and specific career interests.

The important part is learning how to investigate security activity, not collecting as many certificates as possible. Refer to our SOC Analyst Career Roadmap for more information.

Yes, but only if you are willing to make it meaningful.

A strong professional summary can quickly communicate your individual strengths, relevant experience, and the value you offer an employer. A generic summary filled with phrases such as “hardworking cybersecurity professional” will not differentiate you and is likely to be forgotten.

Too many candidates include cookie-cutter summaries that do not say anything valuable about them.

If you include a professional summary, make it specific to the position and use it to show why you are a strong candidate. Make it count. If you cannot write a useful and distinctive summary, that space may be better used elsewhere.

We do not recommend using one universal resume template for every application.

Every resume you submit should be tailored to the specific position. The employer should be able to see a clear connection between the job requirements and your skills, projects, training, certifications, and previous experience.

You can use AI and our community projects to help analyze a job description and customize your resume. However, you must review the finished resume carefully and make sure every statement is accurate.

If you submit the same generic resume to every employer, you are putting yourself at a serious disadvantage.

We generally recommend beginning serious job applications after completing the foundational and hands-on training identified in our SOC Analyst Career Roadmap.

For most candidates, that means completing:

  • One foundational security certification
  • One hands-on SOC certification or equivalent practical training program

You should also be able to demonstrate basic skills in alert triage, log analysis, investigation, documentation, and escalation.

We do not recommend spending large amounts of time submitting applications before you have developed the qualifications employers are requesting. That time may be better spent studying and building practical skills.

Once you have the recommended foundation, begin applying while continuing to learn, build projects, improve your resume, and expand your professional network.

Refer to our SOC Analyst Career Roadmap for a more complete readiness checklist.

No. You do not need dozens of projects. You need a few excellent projects that directly relate to the position you want.

Cookie-cutter projects that follow a tutorial step by step usually add little value to a resume. Hiring managers have seen the same projects many times, and completing one does not necessarily prove that you can apply the skills independently.

Strong projects should:

  • Reflect work performed in the target role
  • Solve a realistic workplace problem
  • Demonstrate your thought process
  • Produce something an organization could use
  • Include clear documentation
  • Explain what you learned

For someone pursuing a SOC analyst position, strong projects could include:

  • Creating an incident response playbook
  • Developing a guide for investigating a specific type of alert
  • Investigating sample security events and writing professional case reports
  • Creating useful detections with testing, tuning, and documented limitations
  • Mapping an investigation to its required log sources and escalation criteria

Setting up a virtual machine or installing a SIEM may help you learn foundational skills, but the setup alone is not a particularly strong SOC analyst project. Show what you can investigate, detect, document, or improve using the technology.

Avoid creating brittle detections that only work under perfect conditions. A strong detection project should explain the logic, required data, testing process, possible false positives, limitations, and opportunities for improvement.

If you have a project idea, pitch it in the community. We can tell you what we think and suggest ways to make it stronger.

No. A college degree is not required for every cybersecurity position.

However, having a degree can strengthen your resume and make you eligible for more opportunities. Some employers use a four-year degree as a screening requirement. Without one, you may be excluded from consideration even if you possess the necessary skills.

A degree does not guarantee employment, and not having one does not prevent you from building a cybersecurity career. It is one factor among several, including:

  • Practical skills
  • Certifications
  • Professional experience
  • Projects
  • Communication ability
  • Professional connections

A degree is not universally required, but it can expand the number of positions for which you qualify.

No, but an internship can help significantly.

If you are pursuing an associate’s or bachelor’s degree, we strongly encourage you to apply for internships. An internship can provide:

  • Professional experience
  • Practical skills
  • Workplace references
  • Professional connections
  • Evidence that you can contribute within a real organization

An internship can also help you avoid graduating with an education but no relevant professional experience.

It is not mandatory, but it can make you a much stronger candidate for your first full-time position.

A master’s degree can help in some situations, but we generally do not recommend pursuing one solely to land your first cybersecurity job.

For most entry-level candidates, that time and money may be better invested in:

  • Developing practical skills
  • Completing targeted certifications
  • Building strong, job-relevant projects
  • Pursuing internships
  • Gaining professional experience

Based on what we have seen in the field, master’s degrees tend to become more valuable for leadership, management, specialized research, teaching, and certain government or corporate positions.

Our general recommendation is to gain experience first. If a master’s degree later supports your career goals, consider completing it through an employer tuition-assistance program. This can make the degree more financially practical and help you avoid unnecessary debt.

Hands-on experience may not be a formal requirement for every position, but it can make you a much stronger candidate.

Consistent lab work and practical training help you develop skills that are difficult to learn through memorization alone. The more job-relevant knowledge you possess when you start, the less foundational material an employer may need to teach you and the faster you may become an effective member of the team.

This can give you a competitive advantage over someone who has similar qualifications but no practical experience.

The key is actually learning the material. You should be able to explain:

  • What you did
  • Why you did it
  • What evidence you examined
  • How you reached your conclusion
  • What went wrong
  • What you learned
  • What you would do differently next time

Memorizing steps long enough to pass an exam or finish a lab is not the same as developing a usable skill. Many candidates memorize information and forget it shortly afterward.

Focus on understanding, repetition, documentation, and applying what you learn in unfamiliar situations.

We generally do not recommend the Certified Ethical Hacker certification for most candidates.

In our opinion, it has fallen out of favor with many practitioners, and its training and assessment do not provide the strongest practical preparation for modern security testing work compared with more hands-on alternatives.

There are exceptions. Some employers, government contracts, or specific job listings may request the CEH. If the positions you are targeting repeatedly list it as a requirement, it may still have value as a resume-screening credential.

Before paying for it yourself, research the positions you want and compare the CEH with more practical options that demonstrate applied skills.

No. SANS training and GIAC certifications are respected, but they are not required to begin a cybersecurity career.

They can also be extremely expensive when paid for personally. Many lower-cost training programs and certifications can teach relevant material and help you develop practical skills.

We generally recommend waiting until an employer is willing to pay for SANS training. Unless a specific opportunity requires a GIAC certification, you can usually find a more financially practical way to develop the skills needed for your first cybersecurity position.
